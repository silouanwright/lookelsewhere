# Digital Eye Strain Evidence and Content Guide

Research current through 2026-08-25. This is a product and editorial reference,
not medical advice. It exists so LookElsewhere can educate responsibly, choose
useful defaults, and avoid turning a wellness tool into a medical product.

## Executive summary

- Digital eye strain is a real cluster of recurrent symptoms associated with
  screen viewing. It is not one disease with one cause.
- Regular breaks, distance focus, blinking, readable text, appropriate vision
  correction, and a sensible workstation are reasonable interventions.
- The American Optometric Association recommends the 20-20-20 rule.
- Evidence does not show that exactly 20 seconds every 20 minutes is a uniquely
  therapeutic dose. The numbers are a memorable convention.
- A reminder is useful only if people leave it enabled. Interruption timing,
  context awareness, and gentle enforcement are therefore health-product
  concerns, not merely UX polish.
- Blue-light filtering lenses are not supported as a reliable treatment for
  short-term digital eye strain. Night color temperature is a separate comfort
  and sleep-hygiene feature, not a replacement for breaks.
- Persistent pain, significant vision changes, or recurring symptoms deserve a
  qualified eye-care professional, not a stricter timer.

## What digital eye strain is

The TFOS Lifestyle report defines digital eye strain as the development or
worsening of recurrent ocular symptoms or signs specifically related to viewing
digital screens. Commonly discussed symptoms include:

- dry, burning, gritty, or irritated eyes;
- tired or heavy eyes;
- blurred or fluctuating vision;
- difficulty refocusing between near and far distances;
- headaches;
- neck, shoulder, or back discomfort associated with the workstation.

Symptoms can come from overlapping mechanisms:

- reduced or incomplete blinking and tear-film instability;
- sustained near accommodation and binocular demand;
- uncorrected or unsuitable vision correction;
- small text or an uncomfortable viewing distance;
- glare, poor contrast, unsuitable brightness, or dry moving air;
- posture and the cognitive demand of the task.

No single timer can diagnose which mechanism applies. Objective measures such
as blink rate are not, by themselves, validated diagnostic tests for digital
eye strain.

## What the 20-20-20 rule says

Every 20 minutes, look at something roughly 20 feet away for at least 20
seconds. Its strengths are memorability, low cost, and a direct cue to change
focus distance and blink.

The [American Optometric Association](https://www.aoa.org/healthy-eyes/eye-and-vision-conditions/computer-vision-syndrome)
recommends the rule to help alleviate digital eye strain. The broader idea is
also consistent with OSHA guidance to look away at distant objects and blink at
regular intervals.

### What the evidence does and does not establish

The exact numbers should not be presented as a clinically proven dose:

- A controlled study of 30 young adults found no significant symptom,
  performance, or accuracy difference among 20-second breaks every 5, 10, 20,
  or 40 minutes during a 40-minute task.
- A two-week intervention in 29 symptomatic computer users reported more
  breaks and improvements in digital-eye-strain and dry-eye symptoms, followed
  by loss of some gains after reminders stopped. Its size and duration limit
  certainty.
- Reviews conclude that regular breaks may help, while larger and better trials
  are still needed to identify ideal timing and duration.

Sources:

- [20-20-20 Rule: Are These Numbers Justified?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36473088/)
- [Testing the 20-20-20 rule over two weeks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35963776/)
- [Review of display use and dry eye](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35122403/)

### Product interpretation

LookElsewhere should treat 20 minutes and 20 seconds as a sensible preset, not
a universal prescription. The product should:

- make the cadence and duration configurable;
- describe 20-20-20 as an established mnemonic recommended by the AOA;
- say the exact interval is not magic;
- emphasize regular distance focus and complete blinking;
- encourage adjustment based on comfort and professional advice;
- never claim to cure, prevent, or diagnose disease.

## Better break behavior

A useful short eye break can be deliberately simple:

1. Stop looking at the display.
2. Focus on something comfortably distant.
3. Blink slowly and completely several times.
4. Relax the jaw and shoulders if that feels useful.
5. Return when the brief interval ends.

Longer breaks can encourage standing, walking, changing posture, or leaving the
workspace. Instructions should remain short enough that the user does not spend
the break reading the screen.

Missed reminders should not stack. Resume the routine at a sensible next point.
A break app should protect a habit, not punish imperfect adherence.

## Workstation guidance worth teaching

OSHA recommends placing the monitor directly in front of the user, generally 20
to 40 inches away, with the top line at or below eye level. Text should be large
enough to read without leaning forward. Glare, uneven lighting, dirty screens,
and direct airflow can worsen comfort.

Useful educational topics:

- increase text size before moving closer;
- place the display perpendicular to windows where practical;
- reduce reflected glare and extreme brightness contrast;
- blink and focus farther away periodically;
- redirect fans or vents blowing toward the eyes;
- use an appropriate prescription for the actual working distance;
- seek an eye examination when symptoms persist or vision changes.

Sources:

- [OSHA monitor guidance](https://www.osha.gov/etools/computer-workstations/components/monitors)
- [OSHA workstation environment guidance](https://www.osha.gov/etools/computer-workstations/workstation-environment)

## Blue light and night color temperature

Do not market LookElsewhere, hyprsunset, Night Shift, or blue-light glasses as a
proven treatment for digital eye strain.

A 2023 Cochrane review of 17 randomized trials found that blue-light filtering
lenses may not reduce short-term eye strain compared with ordinary lenses. It
also found inconclusive evidence around sleep outcomes. Warmer screen color can
still be a legitimate preference or nighttime-comfort feature, but that is a
different claim from treating eye strain.

Source: [Cochrane review of blue-light filtering lenses](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013244_blue-light-filtering-spectacle-lenses-visual-performance-macular-back-part-eye-protection-and)

## Claims guide for the future website

### Safe, supportable language

- “Helps you build a regular look-away habit.”
- “Designed to reduce interruptions while encouraging screen breaks.”
- “Based on the familiar 20-20-20 guideline.”
- “Regular distance focus and blinking may improve comfort for some people.”
- “Choose a cadence that fits your work and guidance from your eye-care
  professional.”
- “Your data stays local.”

### Language requiring qualification

- “Reduces eye strain” should usually become “designed to help reduce” or
  “helps people take breaks commonly recommended for digital eye strain.”
- “Recommended by eye doctors” should name the organization and link the exact
  recommendation.
- “Scientifically proven” should not be used for the exact 20-20-20 schedule.
- Statistics about prevalence must identify the population, measurement method,
  year, and source rather than becoming a floating headline.

### Claims to avoid

- prevents myopia;
- prevents computer vision syndrome;
- cures dry eye, headaches, or vision problems;
- protects the retina from screen blue light;
- guarantees productivity or symptom improvement;
- diagnoses eye strain from keyboard, camera, or blink behavior.

### Editorial rules

- Prefer primary research, systematic reviews, and authoritative guidance.
- Separate evidence, inference, and product opinion explicitly.
- Include publication date and a “reviewed on” date.
- Link claims near the sentence they support.
- Never reproduce a competitor's language, screenshots, or visual identity as
  our own.
- Add a concise medical disclaimer and advice to seek professional care for
  persistent or concerning symptoms.

## Product decisions supported by this research

- Keep 20 minutes and 20 seconds as defaults, not hard requirements.
- Keep short and long breaks distinct.
- Count active use rather than simple wall time.
- Treat natural away time as potentially satisfying a break.
- Avoid stacking missed reminders.
- Prioritize context-sensitive timing because a disabled reminder helps nobody.
- Keep reminder instructions brief and optionally customizable.
- Prefer local, minimal statistics over content surveillance.
- Preserve reduced motion, high contrast, and keyboard accessibility.
- Keep night-light functionality conceptually separate from break scheduling.
